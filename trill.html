Campaign Primer

The Tale of the First Sundering. A story every child in the kingdom grows up hearing. No one agrees on exactly how it happened. Some say the stars vanished. Some say the moon turned crimson. Others claim the earth itself cried out. What every version agrees upon is this: over a thousand years ago, the world nearly ended.

In those ancient days, strange tears opened across the land. The sky split apart as though made of glass, revealing another world beyond. Through those impossible openings came creatures unlike anything the people of the kingdom had ever seen. They were called the Shreeden. Every painting and carving depicts them differently. No one knows which, if any, are accurate.

The old stories insist they were not invaders. They were fleeing. Behind them came horrors from beyond the tears, monsters so terrible that many legends refuse to describe them directly. Some tales say they devoured cities. Others claim they erased people so completely that even their loved ones forgot they had ever existed.

Whatever the truth, the people of the kingdom stood beside the Shreeden. Together they fought a war unlike any before or since. In the end, the enemy was driven back. The tears were sealed. The kingdom was saved. Then the Shreeden disappeared.

From those events grew the Faith of the Veil, one of the oldest religious traditions in the kingdom. Its priests teach that the First Sundering truly happened and that the sacrifice of the Shreeden should never be forgotten. They preserve ancient songs, ceremonies, and relics said to date back to that forgotten age.

Today, life goes on. The kingdom faces more immediate concerns. For years, fighting has continued along the northern border. Officially, the crown says hostile forces threaten the frontier, and every season more soldiers are sent to defend the realm. News from the border is often confusing: villages abandoned, patrols disappearing, strange accounts from frightened survivors.

If there is any truth to those legends, it has long since been lost to time. Or so everyone believes.

Backstory: Trill

Sedgemere

Trill grew up in Sedgemere, a small Seaborne settlement up the river from Sableport, where the water slows and the banks turn to marsh. Ribbet families clustered along the waterline, living off the river and the trade that floated down from the Basin. Trill's family had been there for generations, and the songs had been there longer: work shanties for the tidal harvest, lullabies for tadpoles still growing their legs, calling-songs for when the fog rolled in and someone needed to find their way home.

Nobody in the family ever called them anything but songs.

Trill's family eventually packed up and sailed out from Sableport, chasing a new island, a safer shore. Trill stayed behind. Not out of stubbornness or anger, but because something unnamed held them to the continent, a pull they couldn't explain and didn't try to.

Kingsfall

Trill ended up in the Crown's service, stationed at Kingsfall in the capital city as a Signal-Bearer in the vanguard. Signal-Bearers carry horns, not swords. Their job is to sound warnings, relay coded calls between units, and keep communication lines alive when the dark and the distance swallow everything else. Trill was good at it. Their ribbet ears could pick up vibrations others missed, and their horn carried clean and far.

On their off time, Trill played taverns. Not for the art. For the free drink, the free meal, and the free bed. Their favorite haunt was The Low Lantern in Grayhaven, a dim, warm, dock-adjacent place where the seafolk came in after long hauls. Seafolk know how to drink, and they tip well.

Hopkins

Trill's signal-bearer partner was a ribbet named Hopkins. Highborne, unlike Trill. Hopkins grew up around the Crown, comfortable in halls and archives Trill would never have been allowed to enter. They were assigned together as a pair, and they became the kind of friends who don't need to explain themselves. Always each other's wingman. Always together.

Hopkins was curious in a way that made him dangerous. He had access to old records, crown archives, things most soldiers never saw, and he started noticing patterns. He found something: a written note, maybe pulled from a restricted collection, maybe copied from a wall in some ruin nobody was supposed to enter. He sealed it in a letter and passed it to Trill.

Trill was too afraid to open it. Then Hopkins vanished.

The Ragged Crossing

Trill and Hopkins were on a patrol route near The Ragged Crossing, in the northern borderlands along the Ashen Frontier. Something came out of the dark. It wasn't a soldier. It wasn't anything Trill could name. It made no sound at all.

Hopkins vanished. Not killed. Not captured. Erased. When Trill returned and tried to report it, the officers insisted Hopkins had never existed. No records. No name on the rolls. No one who remembered him.

The Desertion

Trill grabbed their trumpet-horn and slipped away into the night. They headed south, back toward the warmer waters, to hide in plain sight as a quiet, lazy tavern musician who plays lyre and guitar to block out the silence.

Buy Trill a drink and you'll get three songs and a good story. Buy them three drinks and they'll make you feel like the most important person alive. Just don't ask about the trumpet, and don't ask about the letter in the inside pocket of the coat.

The Songs

Trill carries Illiat's Songbook. It belonged to their great-grandmother, and it's old. The pages are water-stained and written in a dialect Trill only partially understands.

Trill can't read the songs out of the book. Not reliably. But sometimes, under pressure, under fear, when something primal and familial rises up from a place deeper than memory, the songs come anyway. A melody Trill doesn't remember learning. A vibration in the throat that wasn't there a moment ago.

What Trill doesn't know: these aren't just songs. The calling-song that reaches across fog and water to find someone isn't folk tradition. The stage presence that locks a room's attention and drowns out every other sound isn't just charisma. The night Hopkins vanished near The Ragged Crossing, something saved Trill: a fragment of an old family melody, hummed under their breath out of terror. Trill doesn't know why it worked. They just know it did.

The Letter

Hopkins's letter is still sealed. As long as it stays closed, Hopkins is real. He existed. He wrote something, and it's here, in Trill's hands, physical proof that a person named Hopkins once lived and thought and cared enough to write something down.

So it stays sealed. For now.

Rules Reference

Making a Roll
Roll 2d12 (one Hope die, one Fear die) + Trait modifier vs. Difficulty. Hope die higher = succeed + gain Hope. Fear die higher = succeed + GM gains Fear. Below Difficulty = failure. Both match + succeed = critical (double damage).
Attacks
Roll 2d12 + weapon Trait vs. Difficulty. On hit, roll damage dice. Below Minor = 0 HP. Minor+ = 1 HP. Major+ = 2 HP. Severe+ = 3 HP.
Taking Damage
Enemy attacks vs. your Evasion. Armor Slots reduce incoming damage. All HP marked = DOWN. Next hit = Death Door.
Hope
Spend 1 for Experiences (+2 to roll) or helping allies. Spend 3 for Make a Scene.
Stress
Mark when abilities or GM require. If full, mark HP instead. Clear via Rally Dice or resting.
Ranges
Melee = sword's reach. Very Close = a few steps. Close = across a room. Far = bowshot. Very Far = edge of visibility.
Rests
Short: clear Armor Slots. Long: clear Armor, reset per-rest abilities (songs, domain cards), choose a downtime move.
Domain Cards
Recall cost = Stress to bring a used card back. Spellcast: 2d12 + Trait vs. Difficulty.
